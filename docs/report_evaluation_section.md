## Why one protocol

A benchmark is only as good as the guarantee that every generator was scored the same way. This section describes that guarantee: one dataset, one fidelity definition, one utility task, one privacy attack suite, applied without modification to every generator Track 1 produced. The three axes — fidelity, utility, privacy — are described in turn below, followed by the headline benchmark and the trade-off it produces. Track 1's Results section interprets what these numbers mean for the generators; this section establishes that the numbers can be trusted to mean the same thing across them.

## Fidelity

Fidelity is scored per column against the real **training** split — the 94 rows a generator actually saw, not the holdout, which is reserved for utility and privacy. Categorical and binary columns are compared by Jensen–Shannon divergence directly; continuous columns are binned into ten quantile-based bins first and compared the same way. A separate correlation-preservation score takes the mean absolute difference between the real and synthetic Pearson correlation matrices, and a per-column Kolmogorov–Smirnov test reports what fraction of numeric columns pass at p ≥ 0.05. Binary columns are deliberately not quantile-binned: doing so collapses a two-valued column into a single bin and reports a JS divergence of zero regardless of the real positive rate, an error caught and fixed early in the project (P-007).

None of these three has a pass/fail threshold, and that is a decision rather than an omission. The protocol originally set one (correlation ≤ 0.10 good, ≤ 0.20 acceptable), but real train compared against real holdout — two samples of the same population, no generator involved — already scores a correlation difference of **0.203**, worse than the proposed failing threshold. No generator could pass against a bar the real data itself misses on a 94-row sample. The same reasoning dropped a utility threshold, described below. Fidelity and correlation are reported as numbers to compare across generators, not numbers to clear.

## Utility

Utility is scored train-on-synthetic, test-on-real (TSTR): a classifier trained on a generator's synthetic data is evaluated on the real, 35-row holdout, and compared against the same classifier trained on real data (train-on-real, test-on-real, TRTR) as a ceiling. The target is in-hospital mortality (`hospital_expire_flag`), chosen over 30-day readmission because the holdout has only 2 readmission positives against 6 for mortality — not enough to measure anything. Two classifiers are used, logistic regression and random forest, scored by AUROC because of the roughly 31/69 class split. The real-data ceiling from these same runs is **0.578** AUROC for logistic regression and **0.737** for random forest.

The original protocol considered a generator's utility acceptable if its TSTR AUROC came within 0.10 of the TRTR ceiling. That threshold was dropped for the same reason as the correlation one: on this holdout, seed-to-seed standard deviation of TSTR AUROC runs **0.15 to 0.22**, wider than the threshold itself, so whether a generator "passed" would be decided by which of the 20 seeds happened to run. Every utility number in this report is a mean over 20 seeds (42–61); none is quoted from a single run.

## Privacy: the attack suite

Privacy is measured by membership inference: whether an attacker, given a record and the synthetic data, can tell if that record was in the generator's training set. The attack is a shadow-model classifier — eight shadow generators fit on resampled subsets of the population, a nearest-neighbour distance computed for each as the signal, and a logistic-regression attacker trained to read membership from that distance. It is run three ways, over three feature sets: four numeric columns (`numeric4`), all 53 features by Gower distance (`gower`), and Jaccard similarity over ICD-9 code sets (`icd9_codes`). The reported privacy score for a generator is the **worst** of the three, not an average, because the weakest attack is uninformative on its own: the four-column attack reads between **0.503 and 0.523** for eight of the nine generators in the study, including TVAE and the regularized diffusion model, which the other two attacks show leaking. A report built on that attack alone would call almost every generator safe.

Two reference points calibrate every attack number. A generator that returns its own training rows unchanged — an exact copy — scores **1.000** on every attack, the ceiling. Real holdout rows the generator never saw score **0.489–0.503**, the floor an attacker achieves by guessing. Every reported attack number in this study falls between those two anchors, which is the check that the attack suite is measuring something rather than producing noise.

The attack design follows the shadow-model literature (Shokri et al.) under a deliberately strong threat model — the attacker knows the generator family and trains shadow generators of the same class — which [`membership_inference_survey.md`](membership_inference_survey.md) cross-checked against 18 sources, including the two EHR-specific benchmarks closest to this project's setting (Yan et al., Chen et al.). Three gaps the survey found against current practice are closed here: true-positive rate reported at low false-positive rate rather than AUROC alone, a per-record exposure report rather than only an aggregate, and a coverage check (`evaluation/synthetic_coverage.py`) that functions as an informal density-based signal. Two gaps remain open: a proper density-based attack, and a release-only attacker that does not require shadow-model access.

## Privacy: does the suite catch generators that actually leak?

A privacy score means nothing until it is shown to detect a generator known, independently, to leak. The study now has two such generators, both identified by Track 1's in-train memorization check before any attack was run: TVAE, which reproduces 52–71% of its rows as near-exact copies, and the 3000-step diffusion model, which reproduces 80%. The suite ranks both above every generator that does not copy. Worst-case membership AUROC is **0.787** for the diffusion model and **0.680** for TVAE, against **0.632–0.656** for the copulas, the floor and both CTGAN configurations. Each of the two is separated from every other arm (Welch's t-test over 20 seeds, p < 0.0001, except TVAE against the tuned copula, p = 0.0002). The low-false-alarm view is starker: allowed to wrongly accuse only 1% of unseen patients, the all-columns attack identifies **36%** of the diffusion model's training patients, 21% of the regularized diffusion model's and 5.8% of TVAE's, against 1.0–4.3% for the rest and a chance rate of 1%.

The same suite also shows where a single attack misleads. The four-column attack rates TVAE at **0.503**, the lowest score of all nine generators, and the regularized diffusion model at 0.507. That diffusion setting is the more instructive case: it passed Track 1's in-train memorization guard (8.4% near-copies, under the 10% threshold), yet its worst-case score of **0.706** is the second-highest in the study, above TVAE's (p < 0.0001). Passing the guard is necessary for privacy here, not sufficient.

The mechanism is visible in the data. TVAE's synthetic rows sit nearest to only **31 of the 94** training rows and the regularized diffusion model's to **25**, against 39–47 for the copulas, the floor and CTGAN: their output collapses onto a minority of patients. The 3000-step diffusion model does the opposite, spreading over **51** training rows while reproducing them closely, which is how it leaks most while looking least collapsed.

A separate leakage channel appears in every generator, independent of architecture. Diagnosis codes seen only once in the training data leak through all nine arms — AUROC **0.821–0.853** against a floor of 0.481 — including both CTGAN configurations, which learned essentially no dependence. The mechanism is structural rather than model-specific: a generator can only emit a code it saw during training, so a code appearing in exactly one training admission identifies that admission whenever it shows up in synthetic output. The copying generators leak through ordinary records instead: per-record exposure correlates with the number of once-seen codes at **−0.53** for the 3000-step diffusion model and −0.01 for TVAE, against 0.12–0.37 for the generators that do not copy. Two mechanisms, both real.

## Attribute inference

A second privacy test asks whether an attacker, given the rest of a synthetic record's columns, can predict a withheld attribute better for training patients than for unseen ones. The gap between those two accuracies — the **member gap** — is the privacy-relevant number, read against `independent_marginals`, which has no column dependence for an attacker to exploit and so defines the no-signal baseline. Three targets are scored (gender, first care unit, age bucket); a fourth, ethnicity, was dropped after one patient was found to hold 15 of the 35 holdout admissions in a single ethnicity category absent from training entirely, which made the attacker's task trivial and uninformative (P-003). The two positive controls appear again here. The 3000-step diffusion model has the largest gap on all three targets — gender **0.286**, first care unit 0.142, age group 0.221, each p < 0.0001 against the floor — and TVAE's gender gap is **0.183** (p = 0.0004), about four times the floor's 0.042. These are 24 uncorrected tests on a small dataset, so single p-values near 0.05 should not be leaned on.

## The benchmark

![Utility against privacy leakage for nine arms over 20 seeds, with ±1 sd whiskers](figures/pareto_frontier.png)

*Figure 1 — mean TSTR AUROC against worst-case membership AUROC, nine arms × 20 seeds. Produced by [`evaluation/plot_pareto.py`](../evaluation/plot_pareto.py) directly from `evaluation.pareto`'s loader, so the figure and the written numbers below come from the same computation.*

Nine generator configurations from the three families the project set out to compare were run through the full protocol above, each over 20 seeds, 180 runs in total: the no-dependence floor, the Gaussian copula at three shrinkage settings, CTGAN at its original and tuned sizes, TVAE, and tabular diffusion at its best-fidelity setting and its only guard-passing one. The five arms run first and the four added later share one environment: three of the first five were rerun on the later date and reproduced to the last digit. The full table is in [`final_evaluation_result.md`](final_evaluation_result.md).

The headline, stated plainly: on 94 training rows, the statistical baseline is the only family that is faithful without copying. Both neural families fail in one of two ways. CTGAN learns no dependence between columns at either size — tuning cut its marginal error from 0.094 to 0.072 and left its correlation error above the floor's. TVAE and the diffusion model reproduce their training patients, and the diffusion model that does so leaks more than any other generator in the study. Mean JS divergence is **0.016** for the copulas and the floor against 0.042 to 0.378 for every neural arm, between 2.6 and 23 times further from the real distributions. The tuned copula (shrinkage 0.10) is the best trade-off: the best correlation preservation of any arm (0.102), utility of 0.686 that cannot be told apart from TVAE's 0.704 (p = 0.68), and a worst-case leakage of 0.656, below TVAE's and both diffusion settings'.

Utility cannot rank the generators, and the reason belongs in the report. The highest prediction scores belong to the two diffusion settings, **0.810** and 0.793 for the random forest, both above the 0.737 the same classifier reaches when trained on real data. They do not beat real data: they generate the wrong share of deaths, **45%** and **79%** against a real 36%, and AUROC only measures ranking, so a classifier trained on data with the wrong rate can still rank the holdout well. TVAE errs the other way, generating **21%** deaths, and still matches the real-data ceiling, because it copies real patients. On a holdout with six deaths the ceiling itself is noisy. Any use of the data that depends on the actual rate of an outcome, such as prevalence, calibration or absolute risk, would be misled by all three.

`evaluation/pareto.py` reduces each generator to its position on fidelity, utility and worst-case privacy, and marks which configurations no other configuration beats on every axis at once. Eight of the nine are on the frontier; the original CTGAN is the exception, dominated by its tuned version in 62% of bootstrap resamples. Eight non-dominated arms do not rank anything, and the sensitivity analysis says why: drop the utility axis and TVAE and both diffusion settings fall from the frontier entirely (frequency 0.00), because every other generator beats them on fidelity and privacy together. They hold their places only through utility, the one axis their copying and wrong priors inflate. Add a correlation axis instead and the tuned copula is on the frontier in every resample.

## What this section does not claim

The results come from one small dataset: 94 training rows against 110 modeled columns, a 35-row holdout with six deaths, and seeds that resample the generators, not the patients. Every p-value is uncorrected and speaks only to seed variation. The neural configurations are Track 1's in-train choices, made once on this data, so the finding is about these architectures at this sample size, not in general. An attack score near 0.5 means these attacks found nothing, not that the data is safe.

Track 1 ran the same nine arms through the same harness on another machine ([`final_comparison_result.md`](final_comparison_result.md)). The two runs agree on every conclusion and on the privacy order, and match closely on most numbers, but not exactly, even for the deterministic copulas; the environmental difference was not identified. Two frontier readings move between the runs and are not leaned on here: TVAE's bootstrap frequency (0.37 there, 0.59 here) and the tuned copula's frequency without the utility axis, which turns on a fidelity gap of 0.0001 to the floor that is not significant in either run.
